Your Funders Each Require Their Own System. Consolidation Was Never Your Decision.

A case manager finishes an intake, then does it again. Same household, same answers, second system. Then a third, because the youth beds answer to a different agency than the shelter beds. Ask a provider carrying more than one funding stream how many times one family gets typed into a database before anyone is housed, and the answer is never once.

The advice that comes back is always the same word. Consolidate.

That advice reads the duplication as an integration failure, the kind of thing an agency could procure its way out of with a better platform and a bigger implementation budget. Read the federal rules that create the duplication and a different picture appears. The number of systems is set by the funders, in regulation, and for most of those systems the agency is not the party that gets to change it.

The Advice to Consolidate Has the Diagnosis Exactly Right

Give the consensus its due first, because it is not lazy thinking. Double entry burns case-manager hours that were supposed to go to clients, and every field typed twice is a chance for two records to disagree. That disagreement surfaces at audit rather than at entry, which is the worst possible moment to find it. HUD wants what the sector wants: the second goal of its Office of Special Needs Assistance Programs Data Strategy is that “Communities operate data systems that allow for accurate, comprehensive, and timely data collection, usage, and reporting” (HUD, HMIS Comparable Database Manual).

Nobody here defends double entry. So the disagreement is about who holds the decision to end it.

HUD's Regulation Assigns One HMIS Per Geographic Area, and a Multi-Jurisdiction Provider Crosses Several

Two sentences of federal regulation set the count. Under 24 CFR 578.7(b)(1), a Continuum of Care must “Designate a single Homeless Management Information System (HMIS) for the geographic area.” Under 24 CFR 576.400(f), an Emergency Solutions Grants recipient must ensure that data on everyone served “are entered into the applicable community-wide HMIS in the area in which those persons and activities are located.”

Read together, the mechanism is plain. The product is chosen by the Continuum through its HMIS Lead, and the obligation to use it follows the geography of the service rather than the identity of the provider. A nonprofit running shelter in a city and rapid re-housing in two surrounding counties enters into three HMIS implementations chosen by three governance bodies, none of which it sat on as the buyer. A provider in exactly that position put it plainly at our booth at the 2026 National Alliance to End Homelessness conference, anonymized here as everywhere in this piece.

“All the jurisdictions that we have, we have to use their system.”

No procurement decision fixes that, because the agency was never the buyer.

Federal Law Forbids Victim Service Providers From Entering Client Data Into HMIS at All

Here the consolidation argument stops being difficult and becomes unlawful. HUD's own Comparable Database Manual states it flatly: “VSPs are prohibited from entering data into HMIS.” The reason is the confidentiality language in the Violence Against Women Act and the Family Violence Prevention and Services Act, which that manual calls “strong, legally codified confidentiality provisions.” A victim service provider instead runs a comparable database, defined by HUD as a relational database meeting every HMIS Data Standard, and the manual closes the obvious shortcut on the way past: “Excel and Google Sheets (spreadsheets) are not relational databases and do not meet the standards to be considered HMIS comparable in nature.”

Then comes the sentence that settles the argument, from 24 CFR 576.400(f): “Information entered into a comparable database must not be entered directly into or provided to an HMIS.” A second wall stands behind it. Under the Comparable Database Manual, “the comparable database implementation may not allow for client information to be shared across multiple providers”, and two victim service providers “may not share information about the same client… even if they are both actively serving the client.”

So an agency running an emergency shelter and a domestic violence program operates two separate client databases by federal design. An integration between them is a compliance violation rather than an unfunded project, and whatever the vendor's API supports has no bearing on whether it is allowed.

Forty-Six Named Federal Funding Streams Can Land on One Project's Books

The plurality is not only geographic. Element 2.06 of the FY 2026 HMIS Data Dictionary lists the funders a project can record against, and it runs to 50 response options. Two are retired for FY 2026, one is “N/A”, and one is a free-text catch-all reading “Local or Other Funding Source (Please Specify)”. That leaves 46 named federal streams, split 30 at HUD, 10 at the Department of Veterans Affairs, and 6 at Health and Human Services.

Those 46 sit alongside the state, local, and philanthropic sources a housing nonprofit assembles to make a program viable, a separate stack with its own reporting expectations and no HMIS obligation at all. The same dictionary then makes the expectation explicit to vendors: “An HMIS must allow projects with multiple funder sources and multiple grants (with potentially different grant terms) from the same funding source to record and store all funding sources for the project.” HUD is describing the normal condition of a provider that has managed to stay funded.

The distance between them is not cosmetic. The Runaway and Homeless Youth program asks for dental health status, whether the young person was formerly a ward of a juvenile justice system, and an aftercare plan. The Department of Veterans Affairs asks for a VAMC station number and a percentage of area median income. The Housing Opportunities for Persons With AIDS program asks for a T-cell count and a viral load. None of those three funders wants the other two's questions answered on its behalf, and one intake form asking all 40 is a worse instrument than three forms asking only what each funder requires. Consolidating the container does nothing here, because the divergence lives in the requirements.

The Same Client Record Has to Land in Four Separate Federal Front Doors

Data entered once still has to be submitted four times, to four agencies, on four schedules nobody reconciled with each other. HUD collects the Annual Performance Report and the ESG Consolidated Annual Performance and Evaluation Report through the Sage HMIS Reporting Repository. The Department of Veterans Affairs requires SSVF grantees to export client-level data to its own HMIS Repository monthly. The Family and Youth Services Bureau runs the RHY-HMIS Repository for de-identified extracts from youth-program grantees. SAMHSA takes the PATH Annual Report through the PATH Data Exchange.

The entry column is identical on every row and the destination column is different on every row. The cadences do not line up either, so a provider carrying HUD and VA money produces a monthly export for one funder and an annual report for the other from the same enrollments. One consolidated system does not collapse four submissions into one, because the four agencies never agreed to receive one.

The Strongest Case for Consolidation Says Integration Was Always the Real Ask

The best version of the opposing argument denies none of the above, and it has three parts worth stating at full strength. First, nobody asked to abolish the mandated systems. The ask was one place where staff type and machines write onward into each required system afterward, which is ordinary integration work that HMIS vendors already publish interfaces for. Second, most of the second systems in the field are mandated by nobody. Across our booth transcripts the second system was a CRM, a separate state HMIS instance, a grants system, a volunteer case-management tool, an outreach platform, a field survey tool, a low-code database, paper, and Excel. Roughly one case in fifteen involved a health record, which is the shape most people assume. Third, HUD's own dictionary anticipates one system carrying many funders, as quoted above.

The second point is correct, and it is the useful one. That accumulated layer genuinely is the agency's to consolidate, and advice aimed there lands. Conceding it relocates the advice rather than restoring it: the instruction becomes consolidate the systems you actually chose, a much smaller and far more achievable project than the one usually proposed.

The first point does not survive 24 CFR 576.400(f). An integration needs a write permission in a system the agency does not own, and the comparable-database rule proves the constraint is legal rather than technical, because it forbids the write no matter what the interface supports. Where the block is a regulation, an integration budget buys nothing. The third point confuses two kinds of plurality. Element 2.06 lets one project inside one HMIS record several funding streams, which is real and useful. Section 578.7(b)(1) still hands the next county its own HMIS.

HUD Pays for a Data Warehouse Precisely Because Subrecipients Use Multiple Software Systems

The most telling evidence sits in HUD's list of things Continuum of Care money may be spent on. Under 24 CFR 578.57(a)(2)(iv), an HMIS Lead may use those funds for “Integrating and warehousing data, including development of a data warehouse for use in aggregating data from subrecipients using multiple software systems.”

HUD wrote that clause knowing the systems would stay plural, and budgeted accordingly. The remedy in the regulation is aggregation rather than replacement, and aggregation is a layer added above the systems you are stuck with. That is the difference between a five-year platform migration nobody can fund and a piece of work that starts this quarter.

Three Tiers of System Choice, and One Tier Belongs to the Agency

Sorting the stack by who decides makes the available moves obvious.

TierWho decidesExamplesWhat the agency can change
MandatedFederal statute or regulationA comparable database for DV work; entry in the HMIS for the area where service was deliveredNothing. Plan around it.
DesignatedThe CoC, through its HMIS LeadWhich HMIS product each geographic area runsInfluence through CoC governance, on the CoC's timetable
ChosenThe agencyThe CRM, the grants tracker, the outreach tool, the spreadsheets, and the record staff work in day to dayEverything, and immediately

Three tiers of system choice, sorted by who holds the decision. Tiers one and two are drawn from 24 CFR 578.7(b)(1), 24 CFR 576.400(f), and HUD's HMIS Comparable Database Manual.

The consolidation conversation almost always aims at tier one and lands nowhere, which is why it recurs every year without resolving. Tier two is real but slow, moving at the speed of a governance body rather than a budget cycle. Tier three holds the decisions actually available, and two of them matter more than the rest.

The first is which system holds the record staff work in, the one where a case is understood rather than reported. Plenty of agencies have never made that choice explicitly, which is how a city data lead answered the question at our booth with a flat “We don't have a central one.” The absence is the answer. The second is what the reporting layer reads from, which is the aggregation HUD already funds, and settling it is what makes a reporting layer that assembles an APR from several source systems worth building rather than a workaround. Both decisions sit downstream of an argument we have made before, that most systems in this sector were built to report rather than built to run.

What to Ask a Vendor, and What to Stop Asking a CoC For

The questions below are the ones that tiering makes answerable. Each replaces a question that sounds rigorous in a procurement meeting and cannot be failed by any vendor.

Ask thisRather than this
Show a case manager completing one intake, with the record landing in the HMIS our CoC designatedCan you replace our HMIS
Which of the 40 funder-specific elements does the product collect natively, and which need configurationIs the product HUD compliant
What happens when one household is enrolled under two funders whose required elements differDoes the product support multiple programs
Produce a Sage-ready APR and a VA Repository export from the same enrollments, in front of usCan the product generate reports
How does the product keep DV program data out of HMIS while still producing an aggregate reportIs the product VAWA compliant
Which of our systems does this replace, and which does it read fromDoes it integrate with everything

Every question on the left has a demonstrable answer, and a product that cannot do the thing cannot fake the demonstration. The ones on the right can all be answered yes by anyone. Notice what is absent from the left column: any request that a Continuum change the HMIS it designated, or that a funder accept another funder's report. Those asks spend the goodwill of people you need for a decade, and they fail, because neither party has the authority to grant them.

The honest target is one record staff type into, with a reporting layer that feeds each federal front door out of it. That is procurable, it sits inside the agency's own authority, and it requires no funder to change its mind. The duplication left over is the duplication Congress and three federal departments put there, and carrying it deliberately is a different thing from carrying it by accident.
