HMIS Is a Person, Not a Platform

By Sunil Jith S H · For CoC leadership and the RFP-writing layer

In a lot of Continuums, the working knowledge of how the region’s data fits together lives in one person’s head rather than in the platform or in the documentation. Which report was built for which funder. Which export the monthly numbers depend on. Which users were trained on what, and when. When that person resigns, the software keeps running exactly as it did the week before, and the community loses most of what it could do with it.

We heard that happen at a conference booth last July, in two sentences. An analyst left, and the analyst who replaced him learned almost nothing from the one before. Nothing about the platform changed. What changed was that the region’s ability to answer a question nobody had written down in advance now belonged to somebody who started last month.

One conversation is one conversation. In four to five of the roughly 80 to 90 organizations that came through that same booth, the whole data function was one person, in a room nobody enters by accident. The pattern showed up often enough to be worth naming.

So when a Continuum concludes its HMIS is too slow, the evidence for that is usually real. A question asked in March comes back in May. Data quality flags accumulate between reporting cycles. A replacement gets proposed, a procurement opens, and the document that comes out of it prices modules, integrations, hosting, migration, per-seat licensing and five-year total cost of ownership. There is rarely a line in the template for the person who runs the thing, for how many of them the region has, or for what the implementation loses on the day that person resigns.

That gap is why we say the HMIS is a person rather than a platform. In a lot of communities the capability is a human being, and the software is attached to them. HUD’s own cost rule has been saying something close to that for years.

HUD’s Cost Rule Names Six Salary Activities and One Software Line

The federal rule governing what Continuum of Care Program money may buy for HMIS is 24 CFR 578.57. Read its list of eligible costs next to a typical HMIS budget and the rule turns out to permit a good deal more than most of those budgets use.

One line permits “purchasing software or software licenses.” That is the whole line. A few items further down, the same grant may be spent “paying salaries for operating HMIS,” and that line does not stop. It carries six lettered sub-activities: completing data entry, monitoring and reviewing data quality, completing data analysis, reporting to the HMIS Lead, training staff on using the HMIS, and implementing and complying with HMIS requirements.

The asymmetry is the whole point. HUD spends one clause on the product and six sub-paragraphs on the labor, and every one of those six is something a named human being does on a Tuesday. The regulation is describing an operation, and the software is one purchase inside it.

So when a board finds it easier to approve a platform than a post, the obstacle is usually custom and the way the budget lines are drawn, rather than the grant itself. The money is already permitted to buy hours. What most procurement processes lack is a moment where somebody sets the split deliberately, in front of the people who could change it.

Federal Rule Requires One HMIS and One Lead Per Region, Whoever the Vendor Is

24 CFR 578.7(b) tells each Continuum to “designate a single Homeless Management Information System (HMIS) for the geographic area” and to “designate an eligible applicant to manage the Continuum’s HMIS, which will be known as the HMIS Lead.”

One system. One lead. Per region. Whatever else a replacement procurement changes, it does not change that, because the concentration is upstream of the vendor decision and survives it. The procurement swaps the product sitting at the center of a structure designed to funnel an entire region’s data capability through a single organization, and a standard procurement has nowhere to record how many people inside that organization can do the work.

The regulation is sensible. A region running three HMIS instances has three versions of its own count, and HUD has good reason to forbid that. A structure this concentrated is only as resilient as its thinnest point, and very little in a standard procurement is aimed there.

One Analyst Resigned and the Next Analyst Learned Almost Nothing From the First

In July 2026, five of us worked a booth at the National Alliance to End Homelessness conference for three days. Roughly 80 to 90 distinct organizations came through, and roughly is the honest word: the same visitor often spoke to two or three of us, so we removed twelve duplicates we could positively identify and flagged fifteen more we could not resolve.

In four to five of those organizations, the whole data function was one person.

I do everything. I do the HMIS, the data, coordinated entry, prevention, landlord engagement, CoC.

Before that count is used for anything, the method deserves stating. A booth is the most self-selected room in this sector, because nobody wanders into an HMIS vendor’s table by accident. The number describes that room and is not a measurement of the sector. It is also our own tally from our own booth notes, so there is no external source a reader can check it against.

The count is small and the room was biased. What one of those visitors said carries further than the count does, and it is the conversation this piece opened with, word for word.

I had an analyst, he left. My new analyst, she learned almost nothing from the last one.

That sentence describes something no procurement can answer. Speed had nothing to do with it, and neither did a missing feature. A region’s working knowledge of its own data lived in one head and walked out with it, and a new platform arriving the following quarter would have made the year worse, because the migration would have been run by somebody who started last month.

The Case for Buying the Platform Is Stronger Than Most Critics Allow

A better platform genuinely does reduce the administrator’s load, and the reduction is not marginal. Every hour the incumbent system burns on an export-and-reconcile workaround is an hour the right product hands back. Software is a labor multiplier, so buying it lifts the ceiling for every agency in the region at once, while a hire lifts it for one organization until that person resigns. A recurring salary and a procured license are also different kinds of commitment, and a Continuum funded year to year has fair reason to prefer the one it can size in advance.

The governance version of the argument is stronger still. A board can run a procurement. It cannot conjure a data analyst out of a labor market where it competes for the same skill against every health system and county agency within driving distance, at salaries it cannot match. Telling that board its answer is staffing is telling it to pull a lever it does not hold. Buying the platform is the only instrument the structure hands them, and using the instrument you have is usually the right call.

A Multiplier Applied to One Person Is Still One Person

Three things answer that case, and none of the three require the incumbent platform to be good.

A multiplier needs something to multiply. Halving the time a task takes is worth a great deal when four people are doing it, and much less when the number of people is one and the number of tasks is unbounded. Two of the six activities HUD names under that salary line, completing data analysis and training staff on using the HMIS, do not shrink much when the software improves. They are the same work in a better window.

The failure the booth surfaced is discontinuity, and discontinuity is a different problem from throughput. A faster system does not create a second person who understands the implementation, and the absence of that second person is what turns one resignation into a lost year. No product roadmap addresses it, no reference call surfaces it, and no scoring rubric currently in circulation asks about it.

A migration is then paid for in exactly the resource that is short. The person who runs the current HMIS is the person who maps the fields, validates the export, rebuilds the reports, retrains the users and answers every question during parallel running. A replacement, whatever its long-run merits, spends a year of the scarce input before returning any of it. Where the data function is one person, the procurement and the constraint are competing for the same calendar.

None of this says the incumbent platform is fine. We have argued at length that most systems in this sector were built to report rather than to run, and that the reporting architecture is what turns every APR and CAPER cycle into a fire drill. Our own comparison of the major HMIS platforms reaches the same destination the opposing case does, that aligning the system a community already runs usually beats swapping it.

Four Questions That Change What an HMIS RFP Can Find Out

A CoC board and an RFP-writing team hold a procurement lever and mostly do not hold a hiring one, so the useful move is to make the procurement ask something the standard template does not. Four questions do most of the work, and all four can be added to a document already drafted.

Question to addWhat the question testsWhat a weak answer sounds like
How many administrator hours per month does your implementation assume at our size?Whether the vendor has priced the labor at allA range with no denominator, or the word minimal

Four additions to an HMIS RFP, aimed at the labor the document does not currently price. The six salary activities in the fourth row are those enumerated at 24 CFR 578.57(a)(1).

The first question exists because vendor pricing is almost always expressed per seat and per module, which makes the administrator invisible in the total cost of ownership. Ask for the monthly administrator hours the implementation assumes at your scale and the number becomes comparable across bids, which is the only thing scoring needs.

The second matters more than the first, because migration cost is where an accurate five-year model and a fantasy diverge. A vendor claiming the transition is handled has either absorbed the work or moved it onto your staff, and the difference is measured in the hours of the one person who cannot be spared.

The third question rarely makes it into a written RFP, because it reads as an awkward thing to put in a document a vendor will see. Ask it anyway. Reference calls surface satisfaction and rarely surface fragility, so ask what the vendor has watched happen when a community’s single administrator left mid-contract. Most vendors with real time in this sector have seen it at least once, and the ones who can describe what they did next are giving you something a reference call will not.

The fourth turns the cost rule into a scoring instrument. Read the six activities back to each bidder and make them claim reductions one at a time. Data entry and data quality monitoring plausibly shrink with better design. Analysis and training belong to a person regardless, and a bidder claiming otherwise has told you something useful about the rest of the response.

One decision belongs before the RFP rather than inside it. Section 578.57 already permits the grant to pay salaries, so the split between hours and licenses is a governance question with a number attached. Put it in front of the board deliberately, rather than letting the license quote settle it by subtraction.

Ask How Many People in the Region Can Compose a Report Nobody Wrote in Advance

Nothing above says a Continuum should keep a platform it has outgrown. Some communities should replace theirs, and on the published evidence the case for doing it can be a strong one.

What the evidence does say is that the replacement should be chosen against a count somebody has actually taken. Ask how many people in the region can compose a report nobody wrote in advance, then ask how many of those people are the same person. Where the honest answer is one, the platform is the second problem, and running the procurement first tends to spend a year of the one resource the region already had least of.
