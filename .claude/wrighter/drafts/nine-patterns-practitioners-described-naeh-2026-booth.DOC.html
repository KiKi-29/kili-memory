Nine Patterns Practitioners Described at the NAEH 2026 Booth

By Sunil Jith S H · Field notes for practitioners across the stack

In July 2026, five of us worked a booth at the National Alliance to End Homelessness conference for three days: Kiki Sundaramoorthy, Doug Heilbrun, Arvind Vasudevan, Sunil Jith S H and Chrissy Thompson. Case managers, HMIS administrators, data leads, program directors and Continuum of Care staff walked up and told us how the work actually runs where they sit.

Nobody wanders into an HMIS vendor’s table by accident. The people who stopped already had an HMIS problem and had already decided to spend ten minutes of a conference on it. Everything below is a picture of that room, and none of it is a claim about the sector.

Every count below is distinct organizations, deduplicated across all four sets of booth notes, out of roughly 80 to 90 organizations in total. Roughly is the honest word. The same visitor often talked to two or three of us, so we removed twelve duplicates we could positively identify and flagged fifteen more we could not resolve. One of those four sets produced twelve findings, five of them traceable to a single unusually articulate visitor. Where a finding rests on two organizations we say two. Where it rests on one, we say one.

Nine Patterns, From Fifteen Organizations Down to One

Only one organization put a dollar figure on a data problem. The rest of the cost came out as fatigue, workarounds and time, which is harder to put in a budget line and easier to live with for another year.

Fifteen possible duplicates remain unresolved inside that denominator. Source: CUBE84’s own review of its booth conversations, July 2026.

Fifteen Organizations Run a Second System, and About One in Fifteen Is a Health Record

The second system is where the daily work lives. The ones named were a CRM, a separate state HMIS instance, a grants system, a volunteer case-management tool, an outreach platform, a field survey tool, a low-code database, paper, and Excel. About one in fifteen was an electronic health record.

I’m entering an HMIS, I’m entering an Airtable. It’s a whole thing.

It arrives by default, without anybody deciding on it. “Getting my staff up to that has been a challenge, to where they just say — you know what, I’ll just put it in an Excel spreadsheet. And I’m just like, why am I paying all this money for it?” Somebody else needed nine words: “All we have is spreadsheets. That’s all we have.”

The Agency Typing the Data In Rarely Owns the System

By raw count this was our largest cluster, which is why the table carries an estimate of fifteen to twenty rather than a count. We cannot give a deduplicated organization figure for it, because the same people turned up in several of those four sets and we could not resolve them all.

We are inputters. When it comes to exporting data we ask them for the report. We don’t manage the data.

Another visitor put the same thing in two sentences.

I don’t get to choose. I’m just along for the ride.

We assumed these people had misread their own situation. Then we read four published participation agreements of the kind that governs this arrangement, and three of them describe participating agencies as custodians of the data rather than owners, in that word. People were quoting their own agreement accurately. We had not read it.

Those agreements are public documents, and they are described here by type and never named or linked, because some of the bodies that publish them are organizations CUBE84 may work with. A reader who wants to audit this finding cannot do so from this page, which is a real cost of that choice.

Every Report Is Designed From Scratch, and Eight Years In the Canned Ones Still Need Explaining

Every single report is a fight. Nothing is automatically created. You have to say, this is what I want, and then you go in there and you actually design it.

Ten to eleven organizations build every report that way. The canned reports do exist. Reading them is the skill nobody has. “You have to really know the system to understand what some of these basic canned reports are. I’ve been in it at least eight years now, so I know it. But if you were new coming in, good luck.” One person took the CSV out and built a reporting tool, because that was easier.

In Four or Five Organizations the Whole Data Function Is One Person

I do everything. I do the HMIS, the data, coordinated entry, prevention, landlord engagement, CoC. I do everything.

The fragility runs past workload into what walks out when a person leaves. “I had an analyst, he left. My new analyst, she learned almost nothing from the last one. So now I have to hold her hand.”

Six Organizations Cannot Get Staff Into the System, and Not One Named Per-Seat Cost

The reasons are five unrelated things, and cost is not among them.

What blocks accessWhat causes it
Provisioning chainA first email that takes three weeks or never arrives, then a link, a duplicate check, a second email
Permissions modelNo tier for light-touch partners such as medical clinics, who need less access rather than more
Training cohortRuns every so often, and access waits for the next one
Read partitioningSister agencies in one shared instance cannot see each other’s records
License termsRestrictions on reusing the data

One HMIS administrator put the whole list down to layered federal, state and city requirements. Best case on the provisioning chain is “getting access to the system in under a month.” The permissions gap is the one with a client consequence attached: “They don’t necessarily need access to the full spectrum of clients that live in HMIS. But we don’t have a way to plug them into the system.” We questioned four of these organizations closely on cost. Not one named a per-seat price, and two named something that rules cost out.

A Missing Identifier Becomes an Error on the Report and the Wrong Housing List for the Client

Two organizations described the required data elements not fitting the person in front of them, from opposite ends of the problem.

With our unhoused citizens, they’re not going to give you their identifier. So it’s just not going to be collected, it’s going to be a flag. And they still deem it an error.

That is the reporting penalty. The other end is a screening consequence. “We’re having to guess their age. Not everyone is cookie cutter.” A client with a missing identifier can be placed onto a subsidized-housing list they are not eligible for, and nothing alerts anybody at the point of entry.

Nothing in the System Knows Whether Tonight’s Free Bed Is a Bottom Bunk

Every morning we have to call every shelter to say, what is your bed inventory, because HMIS does not track in real time available bed inventory.

The by-name list has the same hole from the other side. A client entering rapid rehousing falls out of coordinated entry, which drops them off the by-name list, so “we have another list we have to have, and it’s manually kept.” We traced that gap through the federal requirements in what HUD’s coordinated entry standards require and where the work falls through.

The sharpest version came from somebody describing what a street outreach worker has to establish before making a single phone call. Whether there is a bed of the right gender at a specific shelter right now. Whether the client meets that shelter’s eligibility. And whether it is a bottom bunk, because the client cannot climb to a top one.

A Funding Notice Moved Roughly 120 Units Out of Permanent Supportive Housing

Five organizations described a funding notice changing the program model instead of the purchase timing. One is moving roughly 120 units of permanent supportive housing into transitional programs and spending the year on that conversion. Another is expanding into new services to meet a notice. Both are a program redrawn to match a document, and the mix of federal, state, local and philanthropic funders these programs draw on means the document can arrive from several directions at once.

One Organization Priced a Broken Data Path, and the Price Was a Funding Award

One organization lost a funding award because data never reached the HMIS. One organization, and three of us heard it separately.

We lost a lot of money because of that. The data was supposed to still be shared, and it wasn’t. Out of twelve counties we were awarded funding for eight, and they had no record. So I don’t know if we’re going to even get that funding again.

This is the only place in three days where the cost of a broken data path was named in money rather than in fatigue.

The Person Who Could Not Buy Walked Up on Purpose

On a cold call, reaching somebody who does not own the decision is a targeting failure. At a booth the same person walks up deliberately and asks for the demo. Seven or more organizations did that. “We’re not the people to talk to about this — but we’re the people that do the work. This would make our lives easier.”

Locally funded government bodies sit beside the Continuum of Care, with their own money and their own systems. Two organizations. “The CoC is working with federal funds. We work with local funds.”

One direct-service provider has built its own coordinated-entry product and cannot deploy it beyond its own agency without permission from the HMIS lead and the lead’s vendor.

A capability already paid for was unknown one level up. Barcode scanning existed in a platform a community had already bought. Meanwhile people were being scanned in with retail scan guns.

Nobody closed a door. Not one organization refused outright in three days. That is selection rather than appetite. People with no interest in a vendor do not walk into a vendor’s booth.

Dissatisfied, engaged, and immobile anyway. We had no name for that state before this. “I hate it, and I think we all do. I just think that everybody’s afraid to leave it.”

An earlier booth produced a smaller version of the same effect, and our notes from a state housing conference in March carry the same bias.

Seven Things We Did Wrong, and the Worst Was Answering the Question for People

Our opening line in nearly every conversation was that data quality is their biggest problem. In one of those four sets it was the first substantive thing said in four of five files. A visitor agreeing with a claim we handed them is not evidence, and in that same set no visitor raised data quality unprompted. The largest thing we thought we were hearing was partly us.

In one of those sets we asked what system the visitor was actually working in only twice across eight conversations. Both times we got a substantive answer. Three other things we walked in believing did not survive the conversations.

What we walked in believingWhat the conversations showed
Data quality is the visitor’s biggest problemOur staff raised it first; in one set no visitor raised it unprompted
The second system is usually a clinical recordUsually a spreadsheet; about one in fifteen was a health record
Agreements lock agencies out of their own dataThree of the four we read call agencies custodians rather than owners
Credentials are rationed by costNo per-seat cost named in any of the four cases we examined closely

The most useful failure is one exchange. One of our people asked a social worker why she could not see her sister agencies’ records, precisely the question that would have explained the constraint, then pivoted to a demo invitation in the same breath. She never answered. We asked the right question once in three days and talked over the answer.

One conversation is missing from our notes entirely. We know because one of our own people said so.

Four Facts a Street Outreach Worker Needs Before One Phone Call, and No System Held All Four

A bed of the right gender. At a specific shelter. Free tonight. In a shelter the client qualifies for, on the bottom bunk, because the client cannot climb to a top one. In three of these organizations somebody phones around every morning and assembles those four facts by hand.

A booth is a poor instrument, and the counts above are what it can carry. It is also the one place in three days where somebody who could not buy anything walked up unprompted and named exactly which four facts were missing.

What Was Removed From Every Quotation

Every quotation above comes from our own notes of these conversations and every count is our own tally, so this piece cites nothing outside itself. Every person, employer, county, city, state and coalition has been taken out. Roles and organization types are what remain.
