Your HMIS Holds a Person and a Housing Program and Cannot Connect Them

By Sunil Jith S H · For CoC leads, HMIS administrators and shelter operations

Somebody at a homeless authority once walked through everything a street outreach worker has to establish before making a single phone call. The worker needs a bed of the right sex, at a named shelter, free that night, in a program the client is genuinely eligible for, and on the bottom bunk, because this particular client cannot climb to a top one. Four facts stand between one person and one placement, and no system in that community holds all four. So every morning somebody at the authority calls every shelter in turn and assembles the answer by hand.

The sector has a name for the missing piece, and the name is too small. When the sector calls it a real-time bed availability problem, the phrase reads as a feature request that some future release will close. Availability is only one of the four facts, though, and the other three go missing by exactly the same mechanism. The record holds a person in full, and it holds a program in full, but nothing inside it joins the two. So the join gets made by a human being on a telephone, and the only trace it leaves behind is a note that somebody was referred.

That claim is testable against HUD’s own documents, which is why every element number and field count below comes from the FY 2026 HMIS Data Standards Manual, the version HUD released in October 2025 and made effective on 1 October 2025.

Two of the Four Facts a Placement Needs Have No HMIS Element at All

A bed of the right sex, free tonight, in a program the client qualifies for, on the bottom bunk.
What one street outreach worker has to establish before a single phone call.

The FY 2026 standards do not treat those four facts symmetrically, and the asymmetry is the finding.

The client’s sex is a person-side fact, and HUD added a dedicated element for it in FY 2026. Element 4.21 Sex is new in this release, and it is collected at record creation for all clients. Under the CoC Program it is required for all components except SSO Coordinated Entry, and its Project Type Applicability list runs through emergency shelter, street outreach, transitional housing, permanent supportive housing, services only, safe haven, homelessness prevention and rapid re-housing without naming Coordinated Entry once. So the one element that carries that fact is excused in precisely the component where the referral gets made. The program side has no counterpart at all, because element 2.07 Bed and Unit Inventory Information splits a project’s beds by Household Type, and the three options are households without children, households with at least one adult and one child, and households with only children.

A bed at a specific shelter, free tonight, is a program-side fact with a timestamp on it, and 2.07 carries the program side without any timestamp at all. Its Collection Point reads “Initial HMIS project set up, reviewed at least annually and updated as needed to reflect changes,” and the manual adds that “minor day-to-day fluctuations need not be recorded.” Total bed inventory is defined as the count “available for occupancy as of the ‘Inventory Start Date’”. What that produces is a capacity figure with a start date attached to it, which is exactly right for the Housing Inventory Count and useless at 9pm.

Eligibility is the fact that belongs to both sides at once, and it has no element anywhere in the standards. HUD does require the rule to exist in writing, under 24 CFR 578.7(a)(9)(i), where the Continuum of Care must “establish and consistently follow written standards” that include “policies and procedures for evaluating individuals’ and families’ eligibility for assistance under this part.” So the rule is mandatory, and the form it takes is prose, because nothing in the data standards holds a machine-readable version of it.

The bottom bunk has no element either, and it is the fact people assume must be recorded somewhere. Element 2.07’s Bed Type field offers three responses, and all three describe procurement rather than furniture: facility-based beds, voucher beds, other beds. HUD does name the underlying need elsewhere, in the guidance attached to the disability elements, where collection is permitted at admission when the information is “needed to determine whether applicants need units with special features.” The standards therefore contemplate a client who needs a unit with special features, while giving the inventory no field in which a special feature can be recorded.

Fifteen Fields in Element 2.07 Describe a Shelter’s Beds, and None of Them Records Tonight

Element 2.07 is a serious piece of design that answers a different question from the one an outreach worker is asking. Its Response Descriptions list fifteen fields. Three of them are dates and identifiers, namely Inventory Start Date, Inventory End Date and CoC Code. Three more are category labels, namely Household Type, Bed Type and Availability, where availability means year-round, seasonal or overflow rather than free or taken. The remaining nine are integers, and every one of them counts capacity: six dedicated-bed fields for chronically homeless veterans, youth veterans, other veterans, chronically homeless youth, other youth and other chronically homeless persons, plus non-dedicated beds, total bed inventory and total unit inventory.

Not one of the fifteen records how many of those beds are empty right now. The manual is explicit about the cadence it wants instead, saying that inventory counts “should be accurately recorded within the last week of each of the months of January, April, July, and October,” and giving its reason in the same sentence, “to satisfy APR and LSA inventory accuracy.” That amounts to four snapshots a year, timed to two reports. More frequent updates for significant changes are “encouraged, but not required.”

None of that is an oversight. A quarterly capacity snapshot is the right instrument for measuring whether a community’s inventory matches its need, and the annual gaps analysis required by 24 CFR 578.7(c)(3) runs on exactly that. The trouble starts when the planning record is the only record, because a worker with a person in front of them then has to query a planning artifact for an operational answer.

HUD’s Own Wording Makes a Recorded Referral the Proof That a Bed Was Open

Element 4.20 Coordinated Entry Event is where the join is supposed to happen, and reading its instructions closely shows what it actually does. The Data Collection Instruction says: “Recording any event in Field 2, Responses 10 through 15, 17 and 18 indicates there is an opening for the client to be housed by the project.” The same logic repeats inside each of those responses, so that against “Referral to Emergency Shelter bed opening” the description reads: “A ‘referral’ indicates there is an opening for the client to be housed by this project.”

Read as a data model, the direction of inference runs backwards from what anyone needs. The system does not hold an opening that a worker can find and then refer into. A worker finds the opening some other way, records a referral, and the recorded referral is what asserts the opening existed. The evidence of a vacancy is the paperwork of having already used it, which makes the morning round of phone calls the real discovery mechanism and leaves the software sitting downstream of it.

Eligibility arrives even later. Element 4.20’s Referral Result field has three responses, and two of them are failures: client accepted, client rejected, provider rejected. The description of the third one says exactly when the eligibility question gets settled: “A provider may determine, after meeting with the client and reviewing eligibility documentation, that a client is not eligible for a project and reject the referral.” The determination lands only once that meeting has happened, so the rule that decided the outcome was knowable before the referral ever went out, and the record learns it afterward, as a rejection code.

Element 4.19 Coordinated Entry Assessment closes the person side in the same shape, because its Prioritization Status field carries only two responses: placed on prioritization list, or not placed on prioritization list. A person is on a list, and which programs that person qualifies for never becomes part of the record. The field-by-field detail of both elements is set out in what HUD’s coordinated entry standards require and where the work falls through.

Every CoC Is Required to Write Down Its Eligibility Rules, and Most Keep Them in a PDF

The eligibility rules are missing from the record rather than from the community, which is a different problem with a different fix.

Section 578.7(a)(9) is unambiguous about their existence. The Continuum of Care must, in consultation with Emergency Solutions Grants recipients, “establish and consistently follow written standards for providing Continuum of Care assistance,” and the minimum contents run to six items, of which the first is eligibility and three more are prioritization policies for transitional housing, rapid re-housing and permanent supportive housing. Every CoC operating lawfully therefore holds a document that answers the outreach worker’s third question, and has committed to following it consistently. That document is mandatory, locally owned, auditable, and in most communities a PDF. A worker on a phone is reconstructing from memory a rule that somebody has already written down, and the provider at the other end of the referral is the only party who applies it.

HUD Described Vacancy Tracking and Eligibility Matching in 2018, and Made Both Optional

The sharpest document in this argument sits outside the data standards. HUD’s Coordinated Entry Management and Data Guide, in Chapter 3 on data systems at page 24, lists the functionalities a CoC might want from whatever system runs its coordinated entry. Thirteen appear, under three headings, and two of them describe the connection layer almost in the words an outreach worker would use.

“Vacancy tracking, so referrals can be made to available units/beds. Some software can support reservation options to ensure staff on the receiving end know to reserve a bed as they wait for the participant to arrive.”

“Automated housing and service options. Produces a list of housing options and supportive services resources with specific eligibility criteria, such as HIV+/AIDS status, to more efficiently identify and refer resources.”

A third entry, listed under coordinated entry management, asks for “a searchable database of housing and supportive services resources that can be maintained by the CoC” holding “eligibility and exclusionary criteria, resource availability, and contact information.” Between them, those three describe a program record that knows its own vacancies and its own rules, which is the whole of what is missing.

The framing around the list is the point. HUD introduces it by saying “although not every system may have or need each one of these functionalities, the policy oversight entity should use this list as a reference in determining the best solution,” which makes the thirteen a reference rather than a requirement. Compare that with the treatment element 2.07 gets, where the manual states that “an HMIS must have an accurate record of Bed and Unit Inventory Information for all continuum residential projects.” One capability is required, validated and reported on. The other is a suggestion inside a guide whose file dates to November 2018.

The Strongest Case for Keeping Eligibility Rules Out of the HMIS Entirely

There is a real argument on the other side and it deserves stating properly, because it is the argument a good CoC director will make in the first thirty seconds.

Who qualifies for what is a local policy decision, and it is supposed to be. Section 578.7(a)(9) puts the eligibility and prioritization standards in the hands of the Continuum, in consultation with its ESG recipients, precisely so a community can weigh chronic homelessness against families with children against fleeing domestic violence in the way that fits its own inventory and its own population. Those weights move. They move when a NOFO changes, when a funder adds a condition, when a project comes online with a dedicated bed requirement, and sometimes mid-year because one grant agreement was renegotiated. A rule set that volatile is a poor candidate for anything hard-coded, and every hour spent maintaining it in software is an hour off a caseload.

The failure mode is worse than the maintenance cost. A system that tells a worker a client is not eligible, and is wrong, has denied that person a bed, and nobody will ever see it happen. A phone call fails loudly. A filter fails silently and at scale. Set against that, a worker who knows the local rules and calls a colleague who also knows them is a robust, if slow, mechanism, and it has the enormous advantage of putting judgment about a human being in the hands of a human being. Hard-coding eligibility, on this view, is how a community builds the wrong thing and then trusts it.

An Eligibility Rule in a Policy Binder Is Already Hard-Coded, Just Where Nobody Can Check It

That case is right about the decision and wrong about the record, and the distinction between the two carries the whole argument.

Deciding who qualifies is policy. It belongs to the Continuum and it should stay there. Writing that decision down in a form something other than a person can read produces a record of a decision already taken, and a community that has published written standards under 578.7(a)(9) has taken the decision and committed it to paper. The live question is therefore where the fixed version sits: in a PDF that no workflow touches, or in a field the referring worker sees at the moment of referral, versioned and owned by the same committee that owns the PDF. A rule in a binder is already hard-coded. It is coded into whoever happens to remember it.

The silent-failure objection points toward a design answer rather than away from one. A connection layer that gates is dangerous, and a connection layer that ranks and warns is not. Surfacing that a client appears to fall outside a program’s written criteria, with the criterion named, and leaving the referral available anyway, gives the worker the thing they currently reconstruct from memory while taking no decision away from them. It also leaves a trace, which the phone call does not. HUD’s own event list already includes “Referral to Non-continuum services: Ineligible for continuum services” and, separately, “Eligible clients who could not be referred to continuum services because there is no availability.” The standards distinguish an eligibility failure from an availability failure. They simply record the distinction after the fact, one client at a time, which means no community can count how often either one happened before a placement was lost.

Building in that order is what CUBE84 argues for, and the argument holds whether or not a product name ever reaches a purchase order. Housing360 is the HMIS, and it appears on HUD’s FY 2026 vendor report validation list at software version 2.0, with the APR CSV validated 12 August 2026 and the CAPER CSV validated 21 August 2026, which is a table any reader can check for themselves. Care360 carries the coordinated entry work alongside it. The reason two records are involved at all is argued in most housing systems were built to report, not to run, and traced through the daily work in how Salesforce supports housing nonprofits alongside HMIS.

Three Questions to Ask Before the Next Annual Bed Inventory Review

Element 2.07 has to be reviewed at least annually, which means every community has a scheduled moment when somebody is already looking at the program record. Three questions turn that review into a test of the connection layer, and none of them needs a vendor in the room.

  • Where does a worker look to find an opening, and is that place the system? If the honest answer is a phone call, a group text or a shared spreadsheet, then the referral recorded in element 4.20 is a receipt for a discovery that happened somewhere else. Count the minutes a day that discovery costs across the team, because that number is the size of the gap.
  • Which document holds the written standards required by 578.7(a)(9), and does any system read it? Find the file, then check its date against the most recent change to a program’s eligibility criteria. A stale file means people are working from the remembered version.
  • When a provider rejects a referral on eligibility, can anyone count how often and against which criterion? Element 4.20 records the rejection, so if the reason cannot be grouped and totaled, a preventable failure is repeating with no evidence that it is a pattern.

The four facts an outreach worker needs are not exotic. Three of them exist somewhere in a community’s records today, and the fourth is written in a policy document that a committee approved. Assembling them is the work, and at the moment the assembling is done by hand, one call at a time, by the person with the least time to spare.
