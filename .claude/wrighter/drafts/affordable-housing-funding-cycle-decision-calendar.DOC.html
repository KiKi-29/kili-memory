The Funding Calendar That Decides When You Can Act

Ask a housing program when it could take on a new system and you will often hear a version of the same sentence: come back once the new CoC NOFO is out and we have been awarded. That sounds like a polite exit. Read it as a schedule, because a program running on restricted money genuinely cannot commit to an operational change before it knows which grants it holds for the coming year.

The difficulty is that the date inside that sentence moves. Fiscal year 2026 moved it twice.

HUD posted the FY 2026 Continuum of Care Program Competition NOFO on June 1, 2026, with $4,040,000,000 available and applications due in e-snaps by 8:00 p.m. ET on August 26, 2026, according to the Grants.gov opportunity listing. On August 7, 2026, the U.S. District Court for the District of Rhode Island set that NOFO aside in its entirety for failing the notice-and-comment requirements of the Administrative Procedure Act, in a challenge brought over a $1.3 billion set-aside for new projects, per the National Alliance to End Homelessness, one of the plaintiffs. The competition stopped with the deadline nineteen days out.

Renewals ran on a separate track, which is worth knowing before anyone assumes the worst about next year. HUD announced more than $2.4 billion in FY 2025 CoC renewal funding for projects with grants expiring in the third and fourth quarters of calendar 2026, per NAHRO’s May 26, 2026 summary. The pause landed on new projects and left existing awards standing.

So the federal deadline everybody watches was never the date gating the decision.

Your local window closes first, and it closes early

Every Continuum of Care runs its own competition inside HUD’s, ranks the projects, and submits one packet. The deadline that decides whether your project is in that packet belongs to your CoC, and it lands weeks ahead of the federal one. In FY 2026 one regional homelessness authority serving a major metropolitan county released its local request for proposals on June 10, 2026 and closed local applications on July 3, 2026 at 11:59 p.m. PT, fifty-four days before HUD’s August 26 date. A program waiting for the NOFO to come out had thirty-two days from HUD’s posting to get a local application in, and the outcome data that application needed had to already exist on July 3.

One CoC’s calendar is one data point. A local process that runs unusually early opens a wider gap than most, so read fifty-four days as an illustration of the shape and get your own CoC’s dates in writing from the collaborative applicant.

Read that gap as your planning horizon. Work that starts when a NOFO appears arrives about a month late for the window that matters, so the fix is unglamorous: keep the evidence current, and the application becomes an export instead of a reconstruction. That is the same discipline that makes reporting calmer, and it is worth settling when your data becomes grant-ready before the next window opens.

The dated cycles worth putting on a wall

Federal formula money runs on a steadier clock than the competition does. HOME, CDBG, ESG and HOPWA dollars reach a jurisdiction through its Consolidated Plan and annual Action Plan, and 24 CFR 91.15 requires that plan to be submitted at least forty-five days before the start of the jurisdiction’s program year, with a hard outer limit. HUD will not accept a submission later than August 16 of the federal fiscal year for which the funds are appropriated, and a jurisdiction that misses August 16 automatically loses the CDBG funds it would otherwise have been entitled to. Before that submission, 24 CFR 91.105 requires the jurisdiction to give residents a comment period of not less than thirty calendar days and to hold at least one public hearing.

That comment period is the most under-used date on this list. It is the moment a program can say on the record, in writing, what the coming year’s priorities ought to fund, months before any application form exists.

Two of those rows are reporting cycles, and they belong here because they consume the same staff. The Annual Performance Report is due within ninety days of the end of your grant operating year and goes through HUD’s Sage repository, which makes those ninety days the worst moment to be changing how data is captured and the best moment to know you need to. If that period reliably turns into a scramble, the specific reasons APR and CAPER reporting runs long are worth settling before the next cycle. The January count is the other fixed point: HUD requires an annual count of people sheltered in emergency shelter, transitional housing and Safe Havens on a single night in January, and a count of unsheltered people every other year in odd-numbered years.

Which window you are actually standing in

The dates only help once you know which one you are closest to, because the useful action changes completely depending on where in the year the conversation happens.

The same squeeze, one layer down: HMIS seats

Restricted money shows up in a second form that looks like a technical limit and is really a budget line. Under 24 CFR 578.7(b) a Continuum designates a single HMIS for its geographic area and names one HMIS Lead to manage it. Under 24 CFR 578.57, purchasing software or software licenses is an eligible CoC Program cost. Put those together and the number of HMIS seats available to your staff is a line item inside a grant budget controlled by one lead agency, so the seat count moves when that award moves.

That is why credential scarcity gets described as a queue. It also explains the workaround so many teams reach for, keeping the real operating picture in spreadsheets beside the official record, which is driven by how many people can log in. In CUBE84’s conversations with housing programs this year, capped HMIS credentials came up as a blocker on its own, separate from any judgement about whether a system was worth having. That is an observation from our own conversations and not a measured figure. The structural point holds either way, and it is why a second layer alongside HMIS gets framed as a capacity question.

What to have ready ninety days out

Working backwards from any of these dates produces roughly the same short list, which is the argument for treating funding cycles as an operating rhythm.

  • An active SAM.gov registration, renewed inside the last 365 days
  • Enrollments, exits and destinations complete for the full reporting period
  • Outcome figures you can produce twice and get the same answer
  • Your CoC’s local timeline in writing, from the collaborative applicant
  • Your jurisdiction’s Action Plan comment dates, which precede its August 16 ceiling
  • A named person responsible for each submission, so it does not rest on one memory

A funding calendar gives you dates to work backwards from, and the preparation each one demands is the preparation that makes the year’s reporting duller. If funding uncertainty is the constant, then reporting built to hold up when funding shifts is what lets you meet each window with evidence that was already there.

The cycles with no national date

Three cycles that belong on this calendar have no single federal date, and a guessed one would be worse than the gap. Competitive 9 percent Low-Income Housing Tax Credit rounds are set state by state in each agency’s Qualified Allocation Plan under IRC section 42(m), so your own state agency’s current QAP is the only reliable answer. The HIC and PIT count submission window is set each year, and HUD’s 2026 submission guidance says only that CoCs must submit “by the posted deadline on the HDX 2.0.” Local HOME and CDBG subrecipient solicitations are issued by each participating jurisdiction on its own schedule, anchored to the Action Plan cycle above.
