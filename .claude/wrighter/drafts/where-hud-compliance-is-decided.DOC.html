What HUD Actually Measures When It Measures Data Quality

Open the report and count the tables. The Table of Questions in HUD's HMIS Programming Specifications for the CoC APR and ESG CAPER, version 1.0, aligned with the FY 2026 HMIS Data Standards, lists 70 distinct report tables. Six of them sit under Q6, Data Quality, and those six decide whether anyone believes the other 64.

Five of the six audit what is written in a field. Q6a covers name, Social Security number, date of birth, and race and ethnicity. Q6b covers veteran status, project start date, relationship to head of household, enrollment CoC and disabling condition. Q6c covers destination and income at start, at annual assessment and at exit. Q6d covers the chronic homelessness elements, and Q6f counts records left open that should have been closed. All five are answerable by looking at a record and judging whether the value in it is right, which is what a pre-submission review is good at.

Q6e works differently, and it is the one worth stopping on. It reads no field values at all. Per the FY 2026 HMIS Standard Reporting Terminology Glossary, Q6e counts records by “the number of days between [project start date] and [date created],” sorted into six buckets: fewer than 0 days, 0 days, 1 to 3 days, 4 to 6 days, 7 to 10 days, and 11 or more days. The same calculation runs again against project exit dates. HUD is grading the gap between the day the thing happened and the day somebody typed it in.

“Timely data entry is critical to ensuring data accuracy and completeness.” — FY 2026 HMIS Standard Reporting Terminology Glossary, Q6. Timeliness

There is no field to correct there and no review week in which that gap narrows. It was set months earlier, by whether your system made entry easy on the day the client walked in. Note the top bucket, which counts records created before the event they describe. HUD says that row “will only contain zeros if all data was entered on or after the date of occurrence,” so a nonzero count is a signature of batch entry after the fact. Q6e is a workflow measurement printed on the same page as everything else.

Eight of the Thirteen Universal Data Elements Are Fixed to a Moment

The same pattern runs through the schema underneath. HUD's FY 2026 HMIS Data Standards, effective October 1, 2025, define 13 Universal Data Elements that every participating project collects regardless of funding source. The manual splits them into two groups by design, and the split is the whole argument in miniature.

Five are Universal Identifier Elements, one and only one per client record: name, Social Security number, date of birth, race and ethnicity, and veteran status. Those belong to the person rather than to an episode, and the manual says that if they are observed to be incorrect or outdated they must be corrected. Fixing one in March for something entered in September is housekeeping.

The other eight are Universal Project Stay Elements, and each carries a Collection Point tied to a specific moment. Five are collected at project start, two at project exit, and Housing Move-In Date is recorded at move-in. Sixteen Common Program Specific Data Elements, numbered 4.02 through 4.21, sit on top of that, each stored against a collection stage of its own. Most of what HUD asks for is anchored to a point in the work, and a reporting cycle arrives long after those points have passed.

The Sentence in the Data Standards That Settles This

If you read one page of the manual, read element 5.03, Data Collection Stage. It is the field that tells the system which moment a record belongs to: project start, project update, annual assessment, or project exit. And it contains a sentence that closes the argument.

“The Data Collection Stage may not be inferred from the ‘Information Date.’” — FY 2026 HMIS Data Standards Manual, element 5.03

HUD is stating, inside its own schema, that compliance cannot be reconstructed from the data afterward. The stage has to be recorded as the work happens, because no later process may work it out from the dates. That is a design requirement, binding on the software rather than on the person assembling the report.

The annual assessment shows what that costs. Records required at annual assessment must carry an Information Date no more than 30 days before or after the anniversary of the head of household's Project Start Date, and one and only one such record per element inside that 60-day window. So every household carries its own recurring deadline, keyed to its own enrollment date, and the APR has a row counting “adult stayers without required annual assessment.” A calendar with 200 households on it is a scheduling system, and either your software runs it or a person does.

What a Report-Time Fix Can Reach

None of this makes pre-submission review pointless. Review is where genuine value errors get caught, and the four difficulties described above — scattered data, manual assembly, unclear rules, and a long final check — are all real. The useful division is between the errors a review can still reach and the ones that closed months ago.

The rows marked no come back every cycle in the same shape, because the correction happens downstream of the cause and each cycle re-earns the same finding. Teams reading the same Q6e distribution three years running are usually looking at an entry workflow nobody has changed, and there is a real staff cost to fragmented systems that shows up long before the report does.

Four Design Questions to Ask Before the Next Cycle

These separate a reporting problem from a design problem, and none of them can be answered during an APR window.

  • Where does a project start get recorded, and how far is that from the conversation? Every hop between the two shows up in Q6e as days.
  • What tells a case manager that a household's annual assessment window opens next week? If the answer is a spreadsheet somebody maintains, the control is a person.
  • When a required field is skipped, does anyone find out that day or that quarter? Validating at entry and validating at export produce very different Q6b numbers.
  • How many places does one client's information get typed? Duplicate entry turns a busy week into an entry lag.

Answering those four is design work, and it sits alongside the reporting layer. HMIS is the system of record HUD requires. The operational layer is where intake, referral, follow-up and document chasing actually happen, and we have written before about how a Salesforce layer works alongside HMIS rather than in place of it. The same architectural point runs through our webinar on housing systems that were built to report rather than to run.

Reporting Gets Calmer When the Workflow Carries the Compliance

A reporting cycle is an audit of the previous twelve months of workflow, printed as tables. Q6e says so in six buckets of days. Element 5.03 says so by refusing to let any later process infer the stage. The annual assessment window says so by attaching a deadline to each household's enrollment date rather than to your fiscal calendar.

So the useful work happens between cycles. Move validation to the moment of entry, put the anniversary dates in a system that raises them, and cut the number of places a case manager types the same thing. Review week then gets shorter for a reason you can name: there is less left to find. A process that merely feels calmer produces the same Q6e distribution it produced last year.

Reporting will still take work. It will stop being the week that discovers what the year did.
