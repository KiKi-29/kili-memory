## Source: JTBD Framework + Webinar Transcript (Mar 5, 2026)

## Sector Overview

Housing and homeless organizations help individuals move from crisis to stability — shelter, case management, housing placement, and program reporting. They operate on **restricted funding** (HUD grants, state/city funding, foundation grants), which means every purchase decision is weighed against risk to funding continuity, not ROI or growth.

The technology stack in this sector has two mandatory layers:
- **HMIS** (Homeless Management Information System) — government-mandated database for HUD reporting. Tracks numbers: how many people use shelters, find housing, return to homelessness, what services they receive.
- **Salesforce** — sits alongside HMIS and handles the daily care work: tasks, follow-ups, alerts, field access, team notes, full client picture.

**Key positioning truth:** "HMIS tracks numbers for HUD. Salesforce helps staff actually do the care work."

## Audience Segments

### Segment 1: Case Managers (Job Executor — influencer, not buyer)
The person doing the work. Not a decision-maker but the end user whose pain drives the purchase. They are the most important person to speak to emotionally in content — because their leadership buys based on whether the tool will make staff more reliable, reduce burnout, and reduce compliance risk.

**Core Functional Job:**
Move a client from instability toward stability using programs, services, and data that are often fragmented.

Their real job is not "enter data" or "manage cases." It is to ensure the right support reaches the right person, at the right time, without things falling through the cracks.

**The 8-Step Core Job Cycle:**
1. **Intake / Re-intake** — Conversation first, then form. Often paper, PDF, Google Form, or phone notes. Includes VI-SPDAT or vulnerability assessment.
2. **Client Record Creation / Update** — Search HMIS, check duplicates, create/update profile. Already doing two jobs at once: helping a person + cleaning messy data.
3. **Document Collection** — IDs, income proof, disability verification, consent forms, benefits paperwork. Half admin, half detective work. Tracked in a spreadsheet checklist because HMIS doesn't manage this well.
4. **Service Planning & Referrals** — Shelter, benefits, healthcare, employment, housing. Referrals tracked via email, phone, WhatsApp, partner portals — rarely one system. The Case Manager chases responses and makes sure referrals don't become dead ends.
5. **Housing Navigation** — Identifying options, preparing applications, talking to landlords, managing waitlists, tracking status. Operates like a mini CRM that doesn't exist: client name, unit applied, status, follow-up date, missing documents.
6. **Progress Notes & Documentation** — Recording interactions, services, milestones in HMIS. "Quick notes now, detailed notes later" is standard practice — sticky notes, notebooks, Google Docs, pasted into HMIS later.
7. **Outcome Tracking & Reporting Readiness** — Checking that enrollments, exits, services, and required fields are complete before APR/CAPER cycles. During reporting periods, Case Managers stop serving clients to fix data — this is a known, recurring crisis.
8. **Case Transition / Closure** — Recording exit dates, destination, outcomes. Ensuring the next person can understand the case without starting from zero.

**7 Related Jobs (Parallel, Constant, Exhausting):**
1. **Information Reconstruction** — Hunting across HMIS, spreadsheets, emails, notes, and memory to piece together what's actually happening before they can do anything useful. Exists because the system doesn't preserve context.
2. **Workload & Deadline Management** — Juggling intakes, follow-ups, housing timelines, document expiry, and reporting cut-offs (APR/CAPER) using spreadsheets, calendars, sticky notes. Exists because the system doesn't sequence work.
3. **Proof of Work Creation** — Updating records, fixing data errors, responding to audit questions. Translating real-world actions into system-approved evidence. If skipped, creates future funding risk.
4. **Cross-Team Coordination** — Coordinating with outreach workers, housing providers, compliance staff, partner agencies. Only person who sees the whole client picture. Progress depends on people outside their control.
5. **Exception Handling** — Managing clients who disappear, return, have incomplete documents, system errors, or last-minute rule changes. Runs alongside core work and spikes unpredictably.
6. **Continuity Protection** — Ensuring the case doesn't reset when staff turns over or time passes. Writing notes, keeping trackers updated so the next person (including future self) doesn't start from zero.
7. **Risk & Exposure Management** — Thinking: "Will this come back in an audit?" "Will I be blamed for a system gap?" Over-documenting out of self-protection, not choice.

**Emotional & Social Jobs:**
- Want to feel **in control of their work**, not constantly behind or firefighting
- Want **confidence** that what they say about a client is correct
- Want to feel their effort leads to outcomes, not just more admin
- Want to be seen as a **reliable professional**, not a careless data enterer
- Do NOT want to go home carrying anxiety about unfinished cases or missing data
- Do NOT want to be blamed for system issues outside their control

**Tools they actually use (the real stack):**
- HMIS (official record — unavoidable but painful)
- Spreadsheets (where reality lives: what's stuck, who needs follow-up, what's about to become a problem)
- Email
- Phone/WhatsApp for partner coordination
- Paper forms, printed intake documents
- Sticky notes, notebooks, Google Docs
- Phone scanner apps for documents

### Segment 2: Program Directors / COOs / Executive Directors (The Buyer)
The decision-maker. Does not evaluate tools the way a SaaS buyer does. Evaluates every purchase through the lens of funding protection, compliance confidence, and operational risk.

**The purchase decision question is NOT:** "Is this a good tool?"
**It IS:** "Will this decision help or hurt our funding, audits, reporting, and program outcomes?"

**Money reality:**
- Funding is restricted — HUD grants, state/city contracts, foundation grants
- Every dollar is mentally earmarked elsewhere
- Success is not surplus. Success is not losing money they already have.
- Leadership thinks in funding continuity: "Will this funding be renewed next year?"

**6 Financial Metrics They Use (in priority order):**

1. **Funding Protection** — Does this reduce the risk of funding clawbacks, delays, or reductions caused by data errors, non-compliance, or reporting gaps? If yes, it has financial value even if it doesn't save money on paper.

2. **Cost per Outcome, not Cost per User** — They think in: cost per housed client, cost per successful program exit, cost per compliant report, cost per staff hour saved during reporting. "If we spend this, does it reduce effort, errors, or cost somewhere else that matters?" Efficiency = defensibility.

3. **Staff Capacity as a Cost Centre** — Burned-out Case Managers make mistakes → data issues → audit risk → funding risk. A tool that absorbs admin work justifies higher spend because it stabilises operations.

4. **Reporting Effort and Time Cost** — APR/CAPER cycles are high-stakes, time-intensive events. Entire teams stop normal work to fix data. Leaders care about how long reporting takes, how many people are involved, how many last-minute corrections happen. They want reporting to be predictable, boring, and calm.

5. **Compliance Cost Avoidance** — Non-compliance means delayed funding, grants not renewed, increased scrutiny, reputation damage. Even if a tool costs more upfront, reducing compliance anxiety makes it financially acceptable.

6. **Scalability without Proportional Cost Increase** — "If our caseload increases, does our cost explode or stay manageable?" They want to serve more people without hiring more people just to manage systems.

**How they talk about budget:**
They say "budget" and "pricing" — but they are really weighing **risk vs stability vs effort**. Price objections are usually compliance uncertainty in disguise.

### Segment 3: C-Suite (Executive Directors, CEOs, COOs)
Often overlaps with Segment 2. Additional lens: funder relationships, board accountability, organisational reputation. Care deeply about being able to demonstrate impact to HUD, state agencies, and philanthropic funders. Fear audits, public scrutiny, and staff turnover cascades.

## What This Means for Messaging

**Never lead with:** "Salesforce developer" or "on-demand talent" or "Apex" or "LWC" or any technical framing. This audience does not think in technology terms.

**Lead with the outcomes they're already trying to protect:**
- Funding protection
- Audit confidence
- Staff who can focus on clients, not spreadsheets
- Reporting that doesn't require an all-hands fire drill
- A client record that doesn't reset when someone leaves

**The emotional hook that works:**
"Your case managers are doing 7 jobs at once. Most of those jobs exist because the system doesn't support them. We fix that."

**The financial hook that works:**
"One data error in HMIS can cost you funding you've already earned. We make sure that doesn't happen."

**What they're NOT buying:**
- A Salesforce developer
- Technical capacity
- A staffing solution

**What they ARE buying:**
- Operational stability
- Compliance confidence
- Staff who can do the real work
- Reporting that's predictable, not panic

## Affordable Housing Sub-Sector: Coordinators & Operators

This is a distinct sub-segment from the homeless services audience. Affordable housing operators manage **home purchase programs** (not emergency shelter or rental) — helping income-qualified families buy homes through publicly funded programs. Compliance programs in this space: **CDBG, HOME, LIHTC**.

### Market Research Data (research across affordable housing sector)

- **75%** of affordable housing operators rank operational efficiency as their #1 challenge
- Staff spend **39%** of a 40-hour work week on administrative busywork that doesn't directly serve clients
- **60%** of staff wish they had more time for higher-value work
- **89%** agree technology makes their job easier — when it's built for how they actually work
- **58%** of operators see consolidating or integrating software as a top strategy
- **68%** see improving staff training as a top strategy

**Key competitive context (from AppFolio sector research):**
> "Affordable housing managers are responsible for the same exact thing that conventional property managers are responsible for, but they have an entire added layer of responsibility — program compliance." — Karen Graham, 30-year sector veteran

**Customer voice on consolidation value:**
> "No more separate tracking programs. It cut my time for entering subsidy payments from 1 hour down to 5 minutes." — Binita Tenner, Housing Hub

### Segment: Affordable Housing Coordinators (Primary ICP for Affordable Housing LP)

The coordinator (also called Homeownership Coordinator, Housing Program Coordinator, or Case Manager in affordable housing contexts) is the **champion persona** — they influence the purchase decision upward to leadership. They are the primary audience for the Affordable Housing landing page, not the buyer but the person whose pain drives the decision.

**Core Functional Job:**
Move income-qualified applicants through a complex, compliance-tied homeownership program — from first inquiry to keys in hand — while managing documentation, eligibility verification, and program reporting simultaneously.

**The Coordinator's Day (based on Ashley persona / product walkthrough):**
1. **Dashboard Review** — Morning check of asset performance and portfolio impact; need a single view of what's active, what's stuck, what needs attention
2. **New Application Processing** — Receive inquiry, create record, begin tracking. Currently done across spreadsheets, email, and disconnected tools
3. **Eligibility Calculation** — Income verification, AMI (Area Median Income) limits, affordability thresholds — compliance-critical calculations done manually
4. **Stage Tracking & Follow-Up** — Moving applications through program stages; chasing documents, checking statuses, following up with applicants
5. **Property Matching & 360-Degree View** — Identifying eligible properties, capturing repair/renovation cost estimates, factoring costs into total funding and loan calculations
6. **Document Management** — Income proof, ID verification, consent forms, inspection reports, repair estimates — tracked across email, spreadsheets, and paper
7. **Purchase Coordination** — Managing the final purchase stage; ensuring all compliance documentation is in order before closing
8. **Post-Purchase Impact Tracking** — Recording outcomes: property value, family stability metrics, program reporting data

**Real pain points (unfiltered from coordinator communities):**
- "I'm doing the job of at least two people"
- Questioning own competence when systems fail them — it's the structural problem, not a personal one
- No alert when a document expires or a deadline is approaching
- No audit trail maintained automatically — coordinators carry compliance risk personally
- Switching between 4+ tools to piece together one applicant's status
- AMI and affordability calculations done manually — high-stakes, error-prone

**What they're afraid of:**
- Missing something that blows up at audit time
- A file that got lost between systems
- Being blamed for a system gap that was never their fault

**Emotional & Social Jobs (affordable housing specific):**
- Want to feel confident that no applicant is falling through the cracks
- Want to finish the day knowing every active case is moving forward
- Want to be the coordinator who actually gets families into homes, not just manages paperwork
- Do NOT want to carry anxiety home about whether they caught everything
- Do NOT want to feel like the admin is bigger than the mission

### Messaging Rules for Affordable Housing LP

**Primary audience**: Coordinators (champions) — speak to their daily pain and mission
**Secondary audience**: Leadership — compliance confidence, audit-readiness, staff capacity

**Lead with:**
- The gap between the work they want to do and the work the system forces them to do
- Specific, concrete friction: "AMI calculations," "spreadsheet tracking," "switching between four tools"
- The outcome that matters to them: families getting into homes, not admin efficiency

**Never lead with:**
- Salesforce, Apex, LWC, developer, platform-as-a-service
- "Streamline," "holistic," "end-to-end solution" — too abstract, too vendor-speak
- Features before pain

**Tone:** Direct. Specific. Speaks to the coordinator, not about them. "You're juggling" not "coordinators juggle."

**Strongest headline direction:** "Stop managing spreadsheets. Start moving families home."
**Strongest emotional subhead:** "The admin shouldn't be bigger than the mission."

**CTA:** Book a Demo (low-pressure, value-forward — not urgency-based)

## Objections to Anticipate

| Objection | What It Really Means | How to Address |
|---|---|---|
| "We don't have budget for this" | Funding is restricted and they fear misallocating it | Frame as compliance cost avoidance — one audit correction costs more than the service |
| "We already have HMIS" | They think HMIS does everything | HMIS tracks HUD numbers. Salesforce handles the daily care work. They're not the same job. |
| "We don't have an IT team" | Fear of implementation complexity | CUBE84 manages the Salesforce layer. They don't need IT capacity. |
| "We're already stretched thin" | Staff bandwidth is the real blocker | CUBE84 reduces the burden on staff. The point is to give time back, not add a project. |
| "We need board/funder approval" | Multi-stakeholder purchase | Provide ROI framing in compliance and audit risk language, not technology language |
